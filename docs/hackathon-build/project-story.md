# GLT Lab Project Story

## Short Human Version

GLT Lab began with a very personal question.

I once studied Lie algebra for my university thesis. Then life moved on: I spent fourteen years working in IT, far away from formal academic research. In 2026, while preparing to apply for a master's program in machine learning, I remembered that old mathematical thread and asked a simple question:

> Could Lie algebra have anything to do with transformers?

That question became a research project.

With Codex and GPT-5.6, I could return to ideas I had not touched for years. Concepts that once felt distant became usable again. I could ask questions, write experiments, inspect results, fix mistakes, and keep going. The important part is not that the project is finished. It is not. The important part is that research became possible again.

GLT Lab is my example of something larger: AI can help a person return to a difficult question and begin an independent research path.

The project asks whether linguistic changes, such as turning a statement into a question or shifting emotional tone, leave reusable geometric traces inside transformer representations. If they do, we may eventually understand language models not only as systems trained on words, but also as systems with directions for meaning, feeling, uncertainty, and transformation.

The current results are cautious and preliminary. Some signals are real; some are bounded; some controls show where the idea breaks. GLT Lab keeps all of that visible. It is not a polished theory.

But the bigger message is not "join my research." The bigger message is:

> Start yours.

Maybe your question is about language models. Maybe it is about a disease that has shaped your life for fifteen years. Maybe it is about education, accessibility, climate, pain, housing, farming, or something no institution has had enough time to care about yet. AI does not remove the need for evidence, experts, safety, or humility. But it can help more people understand papers, form hypotheses, write small experiments, organize evidence, and keep moving.

The message is simple:

> You do not need to already be a professor to begin asking scientific questions.

If every curious person spent even two to four hours a week exploring a research direction they truly care about, imagine how much faster progress could move. Some attempts will fail. Some will only clarify what not to do. But eventually, someone somewhere will ask the question in exactly the right way, and that may make life easier for many people.

GLT Lab is built from that belief: not that everyone should study my topic, but that more people should be able to begin studying theirs.

Anya, 32, with love for the new and the unexplained.

## Devpost-Friendly Version

GLT Lab started as a personal return to science.

I studied Lie algebra in university, then spent fourteen years working in IT. When I began preparing for a master's application in machine learning, I wondered whether that old mathematical background could still matter. I asked GPT whether ideas from Lie algebra could be meaningfully connected to transformers, and that question became the beginning of this research program.

With Codex and GPT-5.6, I was able to turn a half-remembered mathematical intuition into working experiments: scripts, controls, result tables, figures, failed runs, stronger reruns, and careful caveats. The project now studies whether linguistic transformations appear as reusable geometric objects in transformer embedding spaces.

GLT Lab is the public-facing version of that work. It is designed as an example of what AI-assisted independent research can look like when it is made visible: a person starts with a question, learns what they need, builds small experiments, keeps careful records, and stays honest about limitations.

The broader hope is that tools like Codex can help more people begin scientific work in areas they care about. You still need curiosity, discipline, expert input, safety, and honesty about what the results do and do not prove. But you no longer need to wait until every prerequisite is perfect before you begin.

This is especially important for problems that people carry for years: chronic illness, education barriers, accessibility problems, family care, environmental stress, or overlooked local issues. AI will not magically solve them. But it can help people read, compare, prototype, document, and ask better questions until one of those questions becomes useful.

## One-Minute Demo Narration

I built GLT Lab because I wanted to show that independent research is becoming possible for many more people.

The project began when I remembered my old university thesis on Lie algebra and asked whether those ideas could apply to transformers. With Codex and GPT-5.6, that question became a working research repository.

GLT asks a simple question: when a sentence changes, does the movement between its embeddings describe the kind of change that happened?

For example, if a statement becomes a question, does that transformation have a reusable direction inside the model?

Here is a focused GPT-2 steering experiment. Without steering, the model repeats the statement. With the question-transformation vector added inside the model, it starts producing question marks. Random, wrong-class, and negative controls do not show the same effect.

This is not a claim that we have solved language. It is a small, reproducible piece of evidence. The repository links the claim to scripts, CSV files, controls, and caveats.

The goal of GLT Lab is bigger than one experiment. It is a personal proof that AI can help someone return to a hard question, learn fast, test carefully, and build a real research trail.

My hope is not that everyone joins this research. My hope is that everyone who has carried a question for years can begin their own.

If more people can spend even a few hours a week asking careful scientific questions, progress becomes less closed and much more alive.

## Important Tone Notes

Use:

- "preliminary"
- "reproducible"
- "bounded result"
- "public research lab"
- "AI-assisted scientific participation"
- "inspect, run, and extend"

Avoid:

- claiming that GLT has proven a complete Lie algebra;
- saying transformers will definitely be changed forever;
- implying that AI replaces scientific discipline;
- implying that AI replaces doctors, scientists, or safety-critical expertise;
- giving medical advice or suggesting self-experimentation;
- presenting emotion steering as solved;
- making the project sound like a finished paper rather than an active research lab.

Stronger but safer wording:

> I hope this line of work can eventually help us understand not only words and meanings in transformer models, but also directions of uncertainty, feeling, and transformation.

Broader safe wording:

> GLT is my example, not the destination. The real idea is that AI can help more people begin serious, careful inquiry into the questions that have shaped their lives.
